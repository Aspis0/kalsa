smartphones run large language models entirely on the device, and every part of the phone's hardware chain shapes how fast and how efficiently that can happen. The processor, the memory system, the battery, the thermal design, and the software stack form a single pipeline in which the weakest link decides the user experience. Understanding this pipeline requires looking at each stage in turn, because a mistake at any one stage can waste energy that no amount of clever software can recover later.  The battery in a typical phone is a lithium-ion cell with a nominal voltage near 3.8 volts and a capacity measured in milliampere-hours. Its chemistry stores energy by moving lithium ions between a graphite anode and a metal-oxide cathode through an electrolyte. The cell performs best in a narrow temperature window, roughly between fifteen and forty degrees Celsius. Below that window the electrolyte becomes more viscous and the effective internal resistance rises, so the voltage sags under load and the fuel gauge misjudges the remaining charge. Above that window the parasitic side reactions that slowly consume the electrolyte accelerate, permanently reducing capacity. A phone that runs a heavy workload on a hot day therefore damages its own battery slightly faster than the same workload on a cool day, even though the user notices nothing except warmth.  The fuel gauge is the small measurement system that estimates state of charge. It integrates the current flowing in and out of the cell, corrects that estimate with a voltage-based model, and reports a percentage. Current is sensed either by a precision shunt resistor or by measuring the magnetic field around a conductor. Integration drifts, so the gauge recalibrates whenever the cell rests near full or near empty. Because the gauge reports a smoothed estimate rather than an instantaneous truth, short bursts of load barely move the displayed percentage, and energy measurements taken over a few seconds can be misleading. Reliable power measurements need windows of tens of seconds or longer, which is why careful benchmarks record charge counters at the start and end of a fixed workload rather than trusting momentary current readings.  Heat is the central constraint of sustained computation in a phone. Unlike a laptop or a desktop, a phone has no fan; it sheds heat only through its chassis, by conduction through the silicon package into the mid-frame and then by convection and radiation from the back cover into the surrounding air. The maximum sustainable power is therefore set by the temperature difference the design can tolerate between the silicon and the environment. Engineers describe this with a thermal resistance figure, and the practical consequence is that a phone can run its fastest core at full speed for only a minute or two before it approaches its skin temperature limit. When a sensor near the display or the battery reports a rising temperature, the operating system reduces clock frequencies, first gently and then aggressively, to keep the surface below about forty-three degrees Celsius, which is the point where prolonged contact becomes uncomfortable for skin.  A modern system-on-chip pairs four large performance cores with four smaller efficiency cores. The efficiency cores run at perhaps a third of the frequency and consume a fraction of the power per instruction, while the performance cores deliver the throughput that interactive tasks need. For matrix arithmetic, the chip also provides three specialized engines. The central processing unit has vector instructions that can multiply small blocks of numbers at respectable speed. The graphics processor has hundreds of small ALUs arranged for parallel work, which suits the giant matrix multiplications of a neural network. The digital signal processor is a different design point altogether: it has far fewer parallel lanes than the GPU, but each lane is wired for dense arithmetic on quantized integers, and the memory path to it is narrow and carefully managed. Because the DSP does only arithmetic and nothing else, it reaches a much higher fraction of its peak performance on a neural network workload than a general-purpose core ever can, and it does so at a fraction of the energy per operation.  Energy per operation is the number that decides battery life during inference. On current silicon, a well-scheduled digital signal processor can evaluate the arithmetic of a quantized network while consuming on the order of a few tenths of a watt, whereas the graphics processor may need several watts for the same work and the central processing unit even more. The ratio matters more than the absolute numbers because heat scales linearly with power. A workload that runs at one watt can be sustained indefinitely by the chassis; the same workload at five watts will hit the thermal ceiling within a minute and be throttled to something slower than the efficient engine would have been in the first place.
