Modern smartphones run large language models entirely on the device, and every part of the phone's hardware chain shapes how fast and how efficiently that can happen. The processor, the memory system, the battery, the thermal design, and the software stack form a single pipeline in which the weakest link decides the user experience. Understanding this pipeline requires looking at each stage in turn, because a mistake at any one stage can waste energy that no amount of clever software can recover later.

The battery in a typical phone is a lithium-ion cell with a nominal voltage near 3.8 volts and a capacity measured in milliampere-hours. Its chemistry stores energy by moving lithium ions between a graphite anode and a metal-oxide cathode through an electrolyte. The cell performs best in a narrow temperature window, roughly between fifteen and forty degrees Celsius. Below that window the electrolyte becomes more viscous and the effective internal resistance rises, so the voltage sags under load and the fuel gauge misjudges the remaining charge. Above that window the parasitic side reactions that slowly consume the electrolyte accelerate, permanently reducing capacity. A phone that runs a heavy workload on a hot day therefore damages its own battery slightly faster than the same workload on a cool day, even though the user notices nothing except warmth.

The fuel gauge is the small measurement system that estimates state of charge. It integrates the current flowing in and out of the cell, corrects that estimate with a voltage-based model, and reports a percentage. Current is sensed either by a precision shunt resistor or by measuring the magnetic field around a conductor. Integration drifts, so the gauge recalibrates whenever the cell rests near full or near empty. Because the gauge reports a smoothed estimate rather than an instantaneous truth, short bursts of load barely move the displayed percentage, and energy measurements taken over a few seconds can be misleading. Reliable power measurements need windows of tens of seconds or longer, which is why careful benchmarks record charge counters at the start and end of a fixed workload rather than trusting momentary current readings.

Heat is the central constraint of sustained computation in a phone. Unlike a laptop or a desktop, a phone has no fan; it sheds heat only through its chassis, by conduction through the silicon package into the mid-frame and then by convection and radiation from the back cover into the surrounding air. The maximum sustainable power is therefore set by the temperature difference the design can tolerate between the silicon and the environment. Engineers describe this with a thermal resistance figure, and the practical consequence is that a phone can run its fastest core at full speed for only a minute or two before it approaches its skin temperature limit. When a sensor near the display or the battery reports a rising temperature, the operating system reduces clock frequencies, first gently and then aggressively, to keep the surface below about forty-three degrees Celsius, which is the point where prolonged contact becomes uncomfortable for skin.

A modern system-on-chip pairs four large performance cores with four smaller efficiency cores. The efficiency cores run at perhaps a third of the frequency and consume a fraction of the power per instruction, while the performance cores deliver the throughput that interactive tasks need. For matrix arithmetic, the chip also provides three specialized engines. The central processing unit has vector instructions that can multiply small blocks of numbers at respectable speed. The graphics processor has hundreds of small ALUs arranged for parallel work, which suits the giant matrix multiplications of a neural network. The digital signal processor is a different design point altogether: it has far fewer parallel lanes than the GPU, but each lane is wired for dense arithmetic on quantized integers, and the memory path to it is narrow and carefully managed. Because the DSP does only arithmetic and nothing else, it reaches a much higher fraction of its peak performance on a neural network workload than a general-purpose core ever can, and it does so at a fraction of the energy per operation.

Energy per operation is the number that decides battery life during inference. On current silicon, a well-scheduled digital signal processor can evaluate the arithmetic of a quantized network while consuming on the order of a few tenths of a watt, whereas the graphics processor may need several watts for the same work and the central processing unit even more. The ratio matters more than the absolute numbers because heat scales linearly with power. A workload that runs at one watt can be sustained indefinitely by the chassis; the same workload at five watts will hit the thermal ceiling within a minute and be throttled to something slower than the efficient engine would have been in the first place. This is why the fastest benchmark result, measured over thirty seconds, is often the wrong guide for a device that must answer questions all day.

Memory bandwidth is the other half of the story. A quantized model of a few billion parameters occupies several gigabytes and must stream its weights from RAM on every token. The arithmetic engines can all compute far faster than the memory can feed them, so the realized speed of a token is often bounded not by multiply-accumulate throughput but by bytes per second into the engine. Different engines see different effective bandwidth: the graphics processor shares its high-bandwidth path with display composition, the DSP has its own tight coupling into a slice of shared memory, and the central processing unit reads through the ordinary cache hierarchy. Whichever engine matches the bandwidth-bound character of the workload with the least overhead wins in practice, and that winner can change with the size of the model, the batch size, and the quantization format.

Quantization itself deserves attention. Storing weights in four-bit or eight-bit integers instead of sixteen-bit floating point shrinks the bytes that must move and multiplies the arithmetic density of the specialized engines. The risk is accuracy: activations can have outliers that four-bit grids represent poorly. Practical schemes keep the most sensitive layers or the accumulation paths at higher precision, so the multiply happens in integers while the accumulation happens in wider registers. The engineering question is always where to spend the extra bits, and the answer is measured with divergence metrics that compare the quantized model's token distributions against the reference model on fixed prompts.

All of this hardware variety is hidden behind a software stack. A runtime library partitions the network graph and assigns each subgraph to an engine, inserts the conversions between layouts and precisions at the boundaries, and schedules the work in chunks small enough that the thermal controller can intervene between them. Above the runtime, a policy layer watches temperatures, battery state, and the fit of the model's working set in memory, and chooses which engine should evaluate the next chunk of the prompt. The prompt evaluation phase, which digests the whole user message before the first token of the answer appears, is the most compute-hungry part of a turn, and it is the phase where choosing the right engine pays the largest dividend in both speed and heat.
